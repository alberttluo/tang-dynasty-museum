Poetry

Three poems, read closely: one from before the rebellion, one from inside it, and one from a mountain estate the rebellion ended the possibility of.

  • 李白 Li Bai, 701–762
  • 杜甫 Du Fu, 712–770
  • 王维 Wang Wei, c. 699–761

Hover or focus a character for its sense in that line, and toggle the tone pattern to see the alternation the form is built on.

Level and oblique are drawn here the way prosody handbooks draw them: an open ring for a level syllable, a filled dot for an oblique one. The marks are categories, not contours. They record which of two classes a syllable fell into, and say nothing about the pitch it was said on.

On tones: the level and oblique marks shown here are reconstructed píng/zè categories for Middle Chinese, the phonology of the Tang period. They are not modern Mandarin tones, and a character's modern tone does not reliably indicate its historical category. This site makes no claim to reproduce eighth-century pronunciation and includes no audio, because Middle Chinese phonology is reconstructed from rhyme dictionaries and comparative evidence rather than heard.

How the form is built

A Tang 近体诗 poem is built to a fixed count. Every line carries the same number of characters — five in all three poems here, seven in the other common measure. Lines pair into couplets, the even-numbered lines carry the rhyme, and within each couplet the level/oblique pattern of one line is set against the other.

Positions two and four are where that pattern is binding. The handbook rule is that the odd positions are free and the even ones must be clear, which is why those two columns are the ones to read first in any of the lattices below.
